## Slide 5
What Is a (Classic) Baseline?
The baseline is a characterization of the energy use and operating conditions during a specified period of time prior to implementation. This “specified period of time” is also known as the baseline period.
The baseline provides the basis for the development of the baseline model against which an energy conservation project’s actual performance is compared.
Exception: Physics models may come afterward for new construction.

## Slide 9
Baseline vs. Baseline Model
The baseline contains all the data required to build a useful baseline model.
The baseline data is collected within a period of time called the baseline period.
The baseline model is the foundation of the counterfactual method. It is adjusted (as necessary) throughout the reporting period.
Example: An office building in New York has different heating and cooling needs throughout the year. When measuring the performance of updates to the facility’s HVAC system, a CMVP should select a baseline period (typically one full year) to capture the effects of seasonal changes.

> *Notes:* Instructor Note: “Coverage factor” is another term for the full range of conditions. ASHRAE requires a coverage factor of at least 90%.

Actual measurements from that period

## Slide 12
Adjustments (Classic)
To ensure we are comparing apples to apples, the baseline model must be adjusted to reflect the current conditions. What results is called the adjusted baseline model.
There are two types of adjustments: routine and non-routine.

## Slide 13
Routine and Non-Routine Adjustments
Routine adjustments reflect expected changes in independent variables—changes in weather from season to season, for instance. They occur often during the reporting period.
By contrast, non-routine adjustments must be made to account for changes to static factors—that is, elements of the baseline that were not expected to change during the reporting period.
Common reasons for non-routine adjustments:
Expansion or renovation of the facility
Addition or removal of equipment
Changes in usage patterns

## Slide 16
Choosing a Common Set of Conditions
It is unlikely that operating conditions—weather, facility usage, etc.—during the reporting period will be identical to those during the baseline period.
For this reason, measurements taken during the two periods CAN be normalized to a common set of conditions so that a valid comparison can take place.

## Slide 17
Avoided vs. Normalized vs. Backcasting
Avoided energy/costs: Baseline model is adjusted to reflect reporting period operating conditions.
Normalized savings: Reporting period energy use and baseline are adjusted to reflect a set of “typical” operating conditions.
Backcasting: Reporting period energy use is adjusted to reflect baseline period operation conditions.
In other words, data from the reporting period is used to create a reporting period model. This is useful when “better” data is available for the reporting period than for the baseline period.

## Slide 18
Example of Backcasting
Baseline period energy use data is only available at the annual level. Baseline independent variable data is available at the required interval.
No useful model of the baseline period can be made, so the CMVP should develop a model using data from the reporting period.
Apply the baseline period independent variable data to the reporting period model and compare the baseline consumption to the adjusted reporting period model.

> *Notes:* Instructor Note: This approach is rarely used. It should be relied on only when avoided energy and normalized don’t work.

## Slide 19
Program Reporting
Realization Rate – (ExAnte vs. ExPost )
A metric that compares the actual observed impact of an energy management activity to the estimated or predicted impact. It is usually expressed as a percentage and helps gauge the effectiveness of ECMs.
Realization Rate = (Actual Impact / Estimated Impact) × 100%
A realization rate of 100% indicates that the actual impact perfectly matched the estimated impact. Values greater than 100% mean actual impact exceeded estimates, while values less than 100% mean the ECM underperformed relative to expectations.

## Slide 22
Key Takeaways
The baseline period should be extensive enough that it is representative of the range of expected operating conditions.
Routine adjustments account for expected changes to independent variables. Non-routine adjustments account for unexpected changes to static factors. (sometimes temporary, sometimes permanent)
Data from the baseline period and reporting period must be considered for possible adjustments (routine and non-routine) so that a valid/fair comparison can be made.

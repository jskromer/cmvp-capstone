## Slide 6
Parameters for Measurement
Once the energy use has been identified for measurement, parameters influencing the energy use may need to be measured as well.
Of these parameters, temperature, flow, and production are arguably the most common.
Other examples of relevant parameters include production, quality of materials, concentration of elements in materials, load, human behavior, equipment condition, humidity, illuminance (lux), and location.

## Slide 7

Sometimes parameters must be combined to form relevant data.
Example: Thermal energy, typically composed of the flow of the energy carrier and the temperature difference of that carrier through a process.

## Slide 9
Types of Metering Systems
Metering systems can be categorized in different ways. They may be either permanent or portable (or temporary). They may also be distinguished by their data communication capabilities:
Hardline data communication capabilities
Wireless data communication capabilities
No data communication capabilities—internal storage or instantaneous readings only

## Slide 11
Types of Portable Metering Systems
Two basic subtypes of portable metering systems:
Handheld instantaneous metering
Metering with data logging
The latter either have no data communication capabilities or communicate data through an internet or mobile network.

## Slide 12
Which Type of Metering to Use?
Use a handheld instantaneous metering system when verification of existing or temporary metering data is required or when developing the requirements for a new permanent or temporary meter.
Use permanent metering when performance assessment is expected to take place over the course of six or more months.
Use metering with hardline communication capabilities when the environment is unsuitable for or not conducive to mobile or other wireless communication protocols.

> *Notes:* Instructor Note: All non-instantaneous metering systems should have backup power and internal data storage to minimize the risk of power or communication loss. Also, be aware that metering systems with internal data storage but no data communication capabilities have a tendency to lose data after a period of about two weeks.

When in doubt, consult the project’s metrologist for a better understanding of metering requirements, setup, and conditions. Metrology is a distinct field of specialization, so if you’re unsure, don’t be afraid to ask for help.

## Slide 13
Accuracy and Credibility
As noted in Module 1, an appropriate balance must be achieved between the reduction of uncertainty through metering and the cost of metering.
When trying to strike this balance, the CMVP must consider the impact of uncertainty on the M&V process as a whole.
With the right balance, stakeholders will be aware of and in agreement about acceptable levels of uncertainty. This can help minimize finger-pointing if results are not as expected.

## Slide 24
Calibration
Calibrating metering system components reduces unknown uncertainty factors.
Calibration should be performed by an accredited metrologist.
Most accrediting bodies are affiliated with the International Laboratory Accreditation Cooperation (ILAC). These affiliations ensure that a metrologist’s accreditation meets global standards.

> *Notes:* Instructor Note: Remember that calibration is meaningless if installation and setup are performed incorrectly. Data is useless if it comes from a meter that very accurately measures the wrong thing.

## Slide 25
Calibration Phases
There are two often confused phases in calibration:
Knowing the error of the measurement instrument, sensor, or logger
Adjusting or fine-tuning the measurement system component to achieve the least amount of error possible (also known as zeroing)

## Slide 26
When Is Calibration Necessary?
After calibration-altering event:
Portable equipment—bump, drop, poor storage, battery problem.
Permanent—power surge or spike, fire, etc.
Otherwise:
Portable equipment—Once every year if in weekly use or once every two years if in monthly (or less frequent) use
Permanent—Can remain calibrated for five years or more if properly installed and protected

## Slide 27
Calibration Verification
Calibration can be verified through an in-use calibration check. This is when a meter is used to measure a known load—such as a resistive heater—and its results are compared against those of a calibrated measurement system of known calibration status.
Portable metering systems of known calibration status can also be used to verify the collaboration of permanent metering systems.

## Slide 28
Data-Related Costs
When data is crucial, the data system becomes crucial as well.
Crucial data systems should have additional safety measures, backup features, and capacity to operate outside normal conditions.
All this comes at a cost.

## Slide 29
Indirect Data-Related Costs
There are also indirect costs associated with data loss.
Data lost due to metering or communication failure can mean …
For the CMVP: wasted professional time, delays in deliverables, and increased rental fees
For the client or other stakeholders: loss of productivity, loss of income, missed deadlines, incorrect invoicing, and even equipment or system damage

## Slide 30
Accounting for All Data-Related Costs
The cost of data consists of three parts:
Material
Communication
Maintenance
Many account for material costs but fail to consider the costs associated with communication and maintenance.
The CMVP must budget for all data-related costs that accrue during the M&V assessment period.

## Slide 31
Safety
Only qualified individuals should install, operate, or handle metering systems—and only when permission to perform the work has been obtained.
Each type of metering installation, resource or material being measured, and environment has its own set of safety protocols and requirements. The CMVP must ensure that these are known, understood, and adhered to.
The CMVP may be held liable for any catastrophic events that occur in the course of M&V activities or in systems with M&V metering installed.

## Slide 32
Safety
As a result of failing to follow safety protocols and requirements, many have lost limbs, eyes, and even their lives while working on small, low-voltage distribution boards to install temporary metering.
Events can become particularly tragic when they involve work with steam, hot water, chemicals, pressurized materials, height, depth, extreme heat or cold, or moving parts.
Always coordinate with the proper site representatives.
If you ever have any concerns, have a specialist perform or assist in performing the work.

## Slide 38
Implications for M&V Practice
Four Ways AMI 2.0 Transforms Measurement and Verification
1. Event-Level M&V
Before: 15-minute interval kWh deltas
After: Heat pump cycle counts, EV charging sessions, compressor duty cycles
Benefit: Verify WHAT changed, not just that energy changed
2. DER Verification
Before: Separate meters required for solar, battery
After: Detect solar production, battery charge/discharge from main meter
Benefit: Reduce metering infrastructure costs
3. Load Flexibility Analytics
Before: Assume which loads are flexible
After: Identify actual flexible vs. inflexible loads
Benefit: Data-driven demand response strategies
4. Retrofit Isolation Without Sub-Meters
Before: Install sub-meters for each ECM ($15k-$25k)
After: Identify HVAC, lighting, equipment changes from main meter
Benefit: 70-90% reduction in M&V metering costs
Bottom Line: AMI 2.0 enables professional-grade M&V at a fraction of traditional metering costs

## Slide 7
Financial / Commercial Risks
•	Over- or under-estimate of savings affecting payments or incentives
•	Cost overruns in metering, data handling, or reporting
•	Uncertainty or errors leading to payment disputes or delays
•	Investment value loss due to baseline or reporting errors
•	Risk of savings manipulation or data fraud

> *Notes:* “On the financial and commercial front, quantifying risks is vital to securing payment, incentives, and project success. Overstating savings may lead to clawbacks or disputes, while cost overruns in M&V can erode project value. Likewise, uncertainty or errors can delay payments and cause friction between parties. Independent review and robust savings calculations serve as best practices for risk mitigation.”

## Slide 8
Legal / Contractual Risks
•	Ambiguous contract language creating disputes
•	Breach of M&V contractual requirements
•	Unenforceable M&V plans in court
•	Disputes over measurement period, boundary, or adjustments
•	Failure to follow contractual dispute-resolution steps

> *Notes:* Instructor Note: “Legal” encompasses contracts as well as disputes between parties.

“Legal and contractual risks center on the enforceability and clarity of M&V plans. Ambiguous language or breach of contract can trigger costly disputes. Always ensure contracts faithfully capture the M&V process, define remedies for nonperformance or dispute, and include third-party resolution where needed. This approach reduces legal uncertainty and helps all parties trust the results.”

## Slide 9
Technical / Physical Risks
•	Meter or sensor failure and uncalibrated instruments
•	Missing, corrupted, or incomplete data records
•	Model selection or specification errors
•	Overlooked interactive effects or non-routine adjustments
•	Insufficient data frequency or duration for statistical confidence

> *Notes:* Instructor Note: “Technical and physical risks arise from failures in measurement instruments, data quality, and inappropriate model selection. These risks are usually the most visible but can also be overlooked, especially during operation. Consistent calibration, validated data management, and rigorous model documentation help prevent these problems and maintain project credibility.”

## Slide 11
Energy Savings Performance Contracting (ESPC)
A financing mechanism used primarily by the public sector to pay for energy-related improvements in buildings and facilities.
Instead of relying on appropriations or traditional borrowing, agencies can partner with energy service companies (ESCOs) to fund and implement upgrades.
The key characteristic of ESPC is that the resulting cost savings (in terms of reduced utility bills) are used to pay for the financing of the upgrades.

## Slide 12
An Example
A performance contract for a project in Dubai must conform to local regulations while also accounting for commercial considerations such as local utility prices and cost of capital. It must also reflect technical characteristics—e.g., what energy assets are used in that region, and what constraints do climate and geography impose?

## Slide 13
ESPC – shared or guaranteed?
Guaranteed Savings:The Energy Service Company (ESCO) guarantees a minimum amount of energy savings. If actual savings fall short, the ESCO pays the difference. The client typically finances the project independently.
Shared Savings:The ESCO finances the project, and both the ESCO and the client share the achieved energy savings. There's no guaranteed minimum, and payments depend entirely on actual savings.
Key Difference:
Guaranteed savings shifts performance risk to the ESCO but financial responsibility to the client.
Shared savings shifts both performance and financial risks primarily to the ESCO, with the client sharing only actual achieved savings.

What Is a Utility Program?
An energy efficiency program sponsored by a utility. Utilities sponsor many energy efficiency programs, for several reasons.
It is typically less expensive to source energy through efficiency measures than through the generation of new energy.
Efficiency measures allow utilities to continue to meet customers’ needs during periods of high demand.
Some national and subnational (e.g., state or municipal) governments require utilities to sponsor energy efficiency programs.
Utility-sector energy efficiency programs save electricity and natural gas at less than half the cost of new energy supplies.
—American Council for an Energy-Efficient Economy

## Slide 20
Concept: Savings By Design Programs
These programs provide incentives to designers to build facilities that are better than the required energy code.
Many have evaluations that require M&V to determine if the newly built buildings are actually operating per the improved design.

## Slide 21
Utility Program Considerations
In utility program contexts, M&V and EM&V play complementary roles. An M&V plan guides how a project or program will be implemented, whereas an EM&V plan guides how a program or portfolio will be evaluated.
Similarly, an implementor will be hired to design and/or carry out an M&V plan, whereas an evaluator will be hired to design and/or carry out the EM&V plan.
In most cases, utilities, regulators, implementors, and evaluators are all involved in M&V and EM&V planning processes.

## Slide 22
Timing
M&V planning usually occurs either at the start of a new utility program or the start of a new program cycle. If modifications to M&V plans are required, they tend to be made at the start of a new program cycle.
Program cycle durations can vary across utilities and regulators. It is important to understand the timing of these cycles to adequately plan for proposed changes to M&V and EM&V plans.

## Slide 26
M&V 2.0
In recent years, advanced metering infrastructure and sophisticated data analytics tools have proliferated. Together, these developments have ushered in “M&V 2.0,” characterized by the following:
Granularity: Advanced metering infrastructure automatically measures energy use data at a granular level (hourly or sub-hourly).
Automated Analysis: Automated analysis of granular data allows for precise and near-real-time performance measurement.

FEMP’s M&V Guidelines
Abu Dhabi M&V Protocol Guidance Document
IPMVP
SANS 50010:2018
These documents all center on the same core concept: the counterfactual method. However, they may vary in other respects because they serve different needs and contexts.

## Slide 39
Cost and Risk
Cost: The budget set aside for M&V can vary significantly depending on a project’s needs, the resources available, and the value of the M&V work.
Should be a central consideration in virtually any M&V project.
Risk: Risk is another important element of a project’s context. Any situation where the results of M&V impact the payment of benefits (settlement) entails risk for the parties paying and receiving those funds.
Addressed primarily in contracts

> *Notes:* Instructor Note: “The value of M&V work”: In some South African contexts, tax incentives apply only when a 10 percent threshold for avoided energy use has been reached. This increases the value of the M&V work significantly.

## Slide 40
Cost and Risk
Each context has a particular risk structure. The cost of performing M&V is directly related to the distribution of risk among stakeholders.
The risks in a utility program are borne by the ratepayer, the implementor, and the customer.
In a performance contract, the facility owner and ESCO share risk.
The CMVP should be aware of a project’s risk structure.

## Slide 42
Scope of Analysis
Depending on the context, M&V activities can range from minimal assessment of project performance to detailed scientific research.
Generally, an increase in analytical complexity means an increase in cost.
Some relatively simple M&V can be performed by technicians, while complicated pre- and post-implementation research is often conducted by PhD holders. A CMVP will fall somewhere in the middle, a “practical” researcher.

## Slide 43
Key Takeaways
The primary contextual considerations for M&V projects are technical, commercial, legal, and regulatory.
A CMVP should develop an M&V plan appropriate for a project’s context.
A CMVP should be able to identify and communicate with all stakeholders.
A CMVP should aim to harmonize expectations to assure mutual agreement on the outcome.

## Slide 11
Buildings Evolve
Anyone involved in Measurement and Verification (M&V) must understand Stewart Brand's concepts in "How Buildings Learn" because M&V relies on accurately modeling and measuring building performance over time. ## Slide 21
Two Approaches to Verification
Understanding the fundamental difference:
Performance Verification
Compare to TARGET / SPECIFICATION
Does equipment meet design specifications?
Is system operating as intended?
Are performance targets achieved?
Compare to: Manufacturer specs, design values, contractual targets
Example:
VFD motor rated at 95% efficiency.
Measurement shows 94.8% efficiency.
Conclusion: ✓ Meets specification (within tolerance)
Counterfactual Method (Savings)
Compare to BASELINE EVIDENCE
What WAS happening before project?
What WOULD HAVE happened without project?
Requires baseline data / model
Compare to: Historical energy use, adjusted baseline model
Example:
Baseline: Building used 500 MWh/year
Post-retrofit: Building uses 400 MWh/year
Conclusion: 100 MWh avoided energy use ('savings')
Key Distinction: Performance Verification = 'Did we build it right?' (target)
Counterfactual Method = 'What changed compared to baseline?' (evidence)

Most M&V techniques rely on an approach called the counterfactual method, which assesses “savings”.

This concept can be applied to other resources as well—for instance, water and gas.

## Slide 30
The Counterfactual Method
How to do it: Compare actual post-implementation performance to “what would have happened” had the project not been implemented. Simple, right?

> *Notes:* Instructor Notes
The first law of ESCO dynamics: no matter what happens, the ESCO might try to prove that more resources would have been used if not for the intervention. 
The only way to ensure that the adjusted baseline is “real”—i.e., not ”gamed”—is to develop and follow a solid M&V plan.

## Slide 31
Counterfactuals – Two Types
Longitudinal
Longitudinal benchmarking compares the energy usage in a fixed period for a building, system, or component to a baseline period of the same length, to determine if performance has deteriorated or improved, to set goals for a building or system, or to monitor for unexpectedly high usage.
Cross-Sectional
Cross-sectional benchmarking is the process of comparing a building’s energy efficiency relative to a peer group. It is the first step to determine if a building has the potential to improve its efficiency. It is usually done at the whole-building level, to assess a building’s overall energy efficiency.
Source: Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Energy Information Handbook: Applications for Energy-Efficient Building Operations

## Slide 32
Modeling
The counterfactual method presupposes a means of comparing expected performance to a project’s actual performance. In other words, the CMVP must construct a model.
Creating a baseline model is just as important as measuring actual energy use before and after a project’s implementation.
Without a baseline model, a CMVP can’t calculate avoided energy use.
Another word for model is “inference”.
All models are wrong, but some are useful.
—George Box, statistician

## Slide 37
Other Uses for Models
Models (physical) can also be used to investigate issues like interactive effects and non-routine adjustments.
Interactive effects: The influence of an ECM on the energy use of other equipment in a facility beyond the measurement boundary (the conceptual line demarcating the space in which measurements of equipment and systems will occur).
Non-routine adjustments: Adjustments that must be made to account for changes to elements of the baseline that were not expected to change during the reporting period.

Sampling: Limited baseline data points, fixture surveys

Reporting Period
Measurement: Meter accuracy in post-retrofit data
Sampling: Spot measurements, verification samples
Modeling: Counterfactual uncertainty follows baseline model uncertainty
Key Concept: Counterfactual uncertainty is a function of Baseline model uncertainty

> *Notes:* "The baseline model has a certain uncertainty based on its CV(RMSE) - that's the 'quality' of the model fit to baseline data.
When we start the reporting period, we're asking: 'Does this model still work?' After 1 month, we have limited evidence. After 12 months, we have strong evidence - twelve successful 'predictions' (actually, representations) that validated the model.
That's why FSU has √months in the denominator. Not because we're getting better at predicting, but because we're accumulating evidence the model still represents reality.
Think of it like this: The model's inherent uncertainty (CV(RMSE)) is fixed - that was determined during baseline. But our confidence that it still applies grows with more reporting data. FSU quantifies that growing confidence."

## Slide 57
Uncertainty Examples in M&V
Example 1: Whole Building Regression (Modeling Uncertainty)
Baseline Model: Energy = 100,000 + 50 × HDD (R² = 0.92, RMSE = 15,000 kWh/month)
CV(RMSE) = RMSE / Mean Energy = 15,000 / 300,000 = 5.0%
Post-retrofit savings: 60,000 kWh/year
Fractional Savings Uncertainty (FSU) ≈ 2 × CV(RMSE) / √(months) = 2 × 5.0% / √12 = 2.9%
Reported savings: 60,000 kWh/year ± 1,740 kWh (at 68% confidence)
Example 2: Lighting Retrofit (Sampling Uncertainty)
Baseline: 500 fixtures, sample 50 fixtures (10%) to measure wattage
Sample mean: 95W, Sample std dev: 12W
Standard error (SE) = σ / √n = 12 / √50 = 1.7W
Confidence interval (95%): 95W ± (1.96 × 1.7) = 95W ± 3.3W → [91.7W, 98.3W]
Baseline uncertainty: ±3.5% from sampling
Total baseline load: 500 fixtures × 95W × 3,000 hrs = 142,500 kWh/yr ± 5,000 kWh
Note: Larger samples reduce uncertainty (n=100 → SE=1.2W, CI=±2.4W)
Example 3: Chiller Retrofit (Measurement Uncertainty)
Measurement equation: Energy = Flow × ΔT × Cp × time
Component uncertainties:
• Flow meter: ±1.5% (calibrated, class 1)
• Temperature sensors: ±0.3°C (at ΔT = 10°C → ±3%)
• Time: negligible
Combined measurement uncertainty (root sum square):
U_total = √[(1.5%)² + (3%)²] = √[0.0225% + 9%] = 3.4%
Baseline measurement: 1,000 MWh/yr ± 34 MWh (baseline period)
Reporting measurement: 850 MWh/yr ± 29 MWh (reporting period)
Savings: 150 MWh/yr, Combined uncertainty: √(34² + 29²) = 45 MWh → Savings = 150 ± 45 MWh (30%)
Key Takeaway: All three uncertainty types must be considered in M&V planning. Larger uncertainties require more conservative M&V approaches or increased measurement rigor.

## Slide 65
Key Takeaways: Uncertainty in M&V
1. Three Types of Uncertainty
Measurement (meter accuracy), Sampling (sample size effects), Modeling (RMSE/CV(RMSE))
All three affect BOTH baseline and reporting periods
Must consider ALL types in M&V planning
2. Counterfactual Uncertainty = Baseline Model Uncertainty
The adjusted baseline model has the SAME uncertainty as the baseline model (with one reading)
Why? Because it IS the baseline model applied to current conditions
This is why baseline model quality (low CV(RMSE)) is critical
3. Uncertainties Combine Using Root Sum Square (RSS)
U_total = √[(U₁)² + (U₂)² + (U₃)²]  |  Example: √[(34)² + (29)²] = 45 MWh
For savings: Small difference of large numbers → high relative uncertainty
Rule of thumb: Savings should be >2× measurement/modeling uncertainty
Typical Uncertainty Values in M&V
Measurement: ±1-3% (revenue-grade meters)  |  Sampling: ±3-10% (depends on sample size)
Modeling: ±5-20% CV(RMSE) (whole building)  |  FSU: Target <10% (good M&V), <20% (acceptable)
Confidence levels: Typically 90-95% in M&V reporting
Bottom Line: Quantify ALL uncertainty sources → Identify dominant source → Focus reduction efforts where they matter most

## Slide 66
Examples of Risks
Stock Market: Investing in individual stocks carries the risk of price volatility. For example, if you buy shares in a tech company, the stock price can fluctuate significantly in response to market news, economic conditions, or company-specific factors.
Market Risk: Market risk, also known as systematic risk, is the risk associated with broader economic factors that affect all investments. For example, an economic recession can lead to a decline in the value of various asset classes, impacting the overall market.
Credit: When lending money, there is a risk that the borrower may default on their loan payments. This is known as credit risk. For instance, when you buy corporate bonds, there is a risk that the issuing company may not be able to meet its debt obligations.
Currency: If you invest in foreign assets or trade internationally, you are exposed to currency risk. Fluctuations in exchange rates can impact the value of your investments. For instance, if you invest in a foreign stock and the value of the foreign currency drops, your investment’s value in your home currency may decrease.

## Slide 67
Examples of Rewards
Stock Market: Historically, the stock market has provided higher returns compared to safer investments like bonds or savings accounts. Investors who accept the volatility and risk of the stock market can potentially earn significant rewards through capital appreciation and dividends.
High-Yield Bonds: High-yield or junk bonds offer higher interest rates compared to investment-grade bonds. Investors who are willing to take on the credit risk associated with these bonds can potentially earn greater interest income.
Venture Capital Investments: Investing in startups and early-stage companies involves high risk, but it can also yield substantial rewards if the company becomes successful. Venture capitalists often seek significant returns on their investments when funding innovative companies.
Options and Derivatives: Options and derivatives can offer the potential for large returns, but they are highly leveraged and come with significant risk. Traders who use these financial instruments can profit from price movements in underlying assets but may also face substantial losses.

## Slide 73
Key Takeaways
The counterfactual method, which compares actual performance observed during the reporting period to a prediction of “what would have happened” had the project not been implemented, is the fundamental theory that drives most M&V protocols and standards.
The other significant approach is performance verification—direct assessment of a system’s actual performance to determine whether it meets specified performance standards.
What distinguishes a CMVP is that a CMVP must exercise skilled professional judgment when conducting M&V.
